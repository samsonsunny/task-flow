A few years ago I managed a website redesign inside my task manager. It started sensibly: a project called "Website redesign" with a handful of steps. A month later I opened it up and found this: **Website redesign** → *Design* → *Homepage* → *Hero section* → *Pick new photo*. Five levels deep. I had built a matryoshka doll out of chores.

Here's the part that stuck with me: I never looked at level four again. Not once. That photo-picking step sat in the dark at the bottom of the tower for weeks while I believed the project was moving, because the top of the tower looked organized.

That app let me nest subtasks infinitely, and I assumed that was a feature. It took me an embarrassingly long time to see what it actually was: an escape hatch from making decisions.

## Why infinite nesting fails

Every serious task manager offers unlimited nesting, and on the demo screen it looks powerful. A tree! Structure! Systems! But trees have a property the demo doesn't show: the further something is from the root, the less light it gets.

In practice, deep nesting breaks your productivity in three quiet ways.

**Nesting becomes planning theater.** Rearranging a hierarchy feels like work. It produces the sensation of progress without any of the substance. When a step turns out to be complicated, nesting lets you file the complexity under something instead of facing the real question: what is the actual next action?

**Depth hides deadlines.** In most apps, a task buried three levels down doesn't show up when you look at your day. You see "Website redesign," tidy and collapsed, while the thing that actually needs doing today — picking the photo — sits invisible underneath. Your daily view lies to you by omission.

**Completion gets fuzzy.** With five levels, what does "almost done" even mean? Nobody checked off anything at levels two through four, but the whole structure gives off a finished vibe. Vague completion is how projects stall at 90% forever.

> As David Allen writes in [*Getting Things Done*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0143422369), "You don't actually do a project; you can only do action steps related to it."

Allen's distinction is sharper than it first appears. Projects aren't things you do — steps are. And if a "step" contains its own sub-steps, it isn't a step. It's a project wearing a step's clothing, and your task list should say so.

> "A step that needs its own steps isn't a step. It's a task in disguise."

## The rule: one level deep

So here is the rule I now hold: **a task can be broken down exactly once.** A subtask cannot have children. Ever.

If breaking something down produces a piece that itself needs breaking down, you don't get to nest again. You have to make a real decision, and it's always one of two:

1. **Promote it.** That piece isn't a subtask — it's a full task in its own right, with its own place on your list and its own claim on a date.
2. **Split differently.** The breakdown was wrong. Regroup so that every leaf of the tree is genuinely actionable.

| | Infinite nesting | One level deep |
|---|---|---|
| What planning forces | Endless filing decisions, deferred | One honest question: is this a task or a step? |
| Visibility | Deep items invisible in daily views | Every item surfaces by its own date |
| Completion | Fuzzy "it looks done" vibes | Fraction on the parent — 2/3 means 2 of 3 steps done |
| Deleting a project | Orphans left behind, sometimes | Steps go with it. No debris |
| On a phone | Pinch-zoom archaeology | Flat rows, readable at a glance |

The table's second column isn't a constraint imposed by a tool. It's a constraint imposed by reality — Allen's reality, where only action steps get done.

## Three rules that keep one level honest

One-level nesting alone isn't enough; badly done, it just flattens the confusion. Four companion behaviors make it actually work. These started as implementation details in [Wednesday Calendar](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/wednesday/id1455779789), but they're worth following no matter what tool you use.

**1. Finishing all the steps does not finish the task.** Check off every subtask and the parent stays open, waiting for you. This is deliberate. The last subtask being done is data; the project being done is a judgment. Keeping the final check in your hands creates a built-in review moment — did we actually ship the redesign, or did we ship it with the footer still broken? Automatic completion steals that inspection from you.

**2. Deleting a parent takes its steps with it.** Nothing lingers. Cancelled project means cancelled project — including the reminders attached to steps you'll never do. Half-deleted hierarchies are how ghost tasks accumulate.

**3. Progress is a fraction, not a forest.** Instead of expandable trees, the parent row simply shows where its steps stand: **1/3**. Two taps tell you everything the five-level tower was hiding, in less space than the tower's title.

Each step also owns its own schedule. A subtask with a date shows up in your day alongside every other task — not trapped under a parent that happened to get dated too. An undated step stays quietly at home with its siblings. Either way, nothing hides.

> 💡 **Product tip:** In Wednesday Calendar, subtasks are added from the editor and behave like full tasks — they can carry notes, priorities, and their own reminders. Parent rows show the completed/total fraction, and finishing the parent sweeps its steps along with it. The one thing the app will never do is complete the project for you.

## Try this today

You don't need a new app to run this audit. Ten minutes, any tool:

1. **Open your most-nested project** — the one you've been avoiding looking inside.
2. **Read the deepest level aloud.** If any item there has children, you've found a task in disguise.
3. **Promote it or regroup.** Make every leaf genuinely doable by someone who doesn't need to ask "what does this mean?"
4. **Give real steps real dates.** If a step matters this week, it gets a date and earns its place in your daily view — not a seat in the audience under its parent.
5. **Delete one tower.** If a branch hasn't been touched in a month, cancel it outright. The relief is immediate and the loss is usually zero.

The deeper your current structures run, the more time step five will save you.
